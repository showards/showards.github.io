    Introduction to Balances

    3 minute read

    For database developers, understanding financial balances is critical, as they form the foundation of an organization’s financial health! A balance, in a database, represents a snapshot of a monetary value at a specific point in time. These values could be a customer’s account balance, the amount of cash on hand, or the amount of an outstanding debt. However, the calculation of a single balance does not give a complete picture or tell the whole story. There is more information necessary to get a fuller picture, including the concepts of cash flow, earned revenue and unearned revenue.

    Cash Flow Statements

    A cash flow statement is a financial document that shows how much cash are moving in and out of an organization over a specific time. Unlike a balance sheet, which is a snapshot at a particular moment, a cash flow statement is a dynamic view of an organization’s financial liquidity. The statement reveals where the money came from and where it went, categorized into three main categories: operating, investing, and financing activities.

    Cash Flow Activities

    The operating activities of an organization are its day-to-day activities, and include both making and spending money. There are inflows of revenue which include: payments from customers, refunds from suppliers, and interest/dividends from investments. There are also cash outflows which include: payments made to suppliers, salaries and wages paid to employees, and payments for operating expenses (rent, utilities, insurance, etc.)

    The main purpose of investing activities is to buy and sell fixed assets, like: plant, property and equipment. Negative balances in this section of a cash flow report often means the organization is investing in itself through major acquisitions, while positive balances in this area may indicate restructuring or other major internal changes.

    The activities detailed in the financing activities section of a cash flow report revolve around an organization’s owners (shareholders) and its creditors (lenders.) Essentially, the financing activities show how an organization raises capital and repays it.

    Unearned vs Earned Revenue

    The distinction between unearned revenue and earned revenue is the cornerstone of accrual-based accounting and is critical for database design. Unearned revenue is money an organization receives in advance for goods or services it has yet to deliver. In a database, this is recorded as a liability. It’s not yet “income” (asset) because the organization still owes a service or product to the customer.

    For example, if a customer pays for a one-year subscription upfront, the full amount is initially unearned income. As each monthly invoice is generated, a portion of the unearned revenue becomes earned as the products or services are provided. Over time, as an organization fulfills its contractual obligations, the unearned revenue liability is decreased and the earned revenue income increases. These processes occur in every business, but are more prominent in “subscription business models:” housing (rent), magazines, online streaming services, and more…

    Putting it Together

    In a complex world of business and finance, understanding a company’s financial standing is paramount for stakeholders ranging from management and investors to creditors and employees. While various reports offer pieces of the puzzle, two statements stand out as fundamentals in financial reporting: the balance sheet and the cash flow statement. These documents provide essential and complementary insights, and offer a dual lens through which to examine a company’s financial health, stability, and operational efficiency.

    Assets represent what the company owns, from tangible items like cash, inventory, and property, plant, and equipment, to intangible assets such as patents or goodwill. Liabilities represent what the company owes to others, including short-term obligations like accounts payable and expenses, and long-term debts like loans and bonds. Equity represents the residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting liabilities: the owners’ stake in the company.
